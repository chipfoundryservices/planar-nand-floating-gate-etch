# Chapter 2: Floating Gate Stack Materials — Tunnel Oxide, Polysilicon, ONO, and Control Gate

## Executive Summary

Every material in the floating gate stack earns its place through a specific electrical or mechanical requirement, and every one of those requirements constrains the etch process that must cut through it. This chapter develops the physical and electrical properties of the five materials (or material systems) that make up the stack — tunnel oxide, floating gate polysilicon, ONO interpoly dielectric, control gate polysilicon, and the silicide/metal cap — with emphasis on the properties that directly determine etch chemistry selection, selectivity requirements, and defect sensitivity, developed in full in Part II. Readers already comfortable with these materials from a device physics perspective should still read this chapter, because the etch-relevant properties emphasized here (plasma etch rate behavior, byproduct volatility, damage sensitivity) are generally not the properties device physics courses emphasize.

---

## Part 1: Tunnel Oxide

### 1.1 Composition and Formation

Tunnel oxide is thermally grown SiO₂, typically 6–9 nm thick in advanced planar NAND generations (thicker, 8–12 nm, in earlier/larger-node generations where retention margin was less pressured by scaling). It is grown, not deposited, because thermal oxidation produces the lowest defect density, most uniform, most reproducible ultra-thin oxide available in high-volume manufacturing — defect density in this layer maps directly and unforgivingly to retention failures.

### 1.2 Why It Must Be Thin, and Why That Is Dangerous

Tunnel oxide thickness is set by two competing requirements pulling in opposite directions:

- **Thin enough to tunnel:** Fowler-Nordheim tunneling current depends exponentially on oxide thickness and applied field; thicker oxide requires higher programming voltages to achieve usable write/erase speeds, which strains peripheral high-voltage circuit design and accelerates oxide wearout
- **Thick enough to retain:** The same tunneling mechanism that allows programming also allows unintended charge leakage; thinner oxide leaks stored charge faster, directly reducing data retention time, and is also more susceptible to trap-assisted tunneling after cycling-induced or etch-induced damage accumulates

This tension means tunnel oxide thickness is already operating near its practical minimum at any given node — there is essentially *no* margin to spend on etch-induced thinning, pinholes, or interface damage. A plasma process that "merely" thins the tunnel oxide by half a nanometer at the floating gate edge — invisible at the wafer-level electrical test — can measurably shift the retention distribution of the array.

### 1.3 Etch-Relevant Properties

| Property | Value / Behavior | Etch Consequence |
|----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Material** | Thermally grown SiO₂ | Etches readily in fluorocarbon chemistries used for ONO (Chapter 7); this is dangerous, not convenient, because it means the floating gate etch (Chapter 8) must stop *before* reaching it with near-zero margin |
| **Thickness** | 6–9 nm (advanced nodes) | A single plasma over-etch step sized for a thicker nominal film can consume the entire tunnel oxide budget |
| **Charge sensitivity** | High — traps generated by energetic ion bombardment or UV/VUV plasma photon flux degrade retention | Drives low-ion-energy, charge-minimizing endpoint and overetch strategy (Chapters 9, 13) |
| **Interface quality** | Si/SiO₂ interface state density must remain low | Physical sputtering damage or residual contamination at this interface during floating gate etch directly degrades channel mobility and subthreshold characteristics |

## Part 2: Floating Gate Polysilicon

### 2.1 Why Polysilicon, Not Single-Crystal Silicon or Metal

The floating gate is deposited polysilicon (typically via low-pressure CVD), not single-crystal silicon, because it must be deposited on top of the tunnel oxide at relatively low thermal budget, and because polysilicon's electrical and etch behavior is well-characterized from decades of gate-electrode manufacturing experience predating NAND. A metal floating gate was explored in research contexts (offering higher work function tunability and eliminating poly depletion effects) but never displaced polysilicon in mainstream planar NAND production, in part because the etch chemistry, selectivity, and damage behavior of polysilicon-on-oxide was already a mature, trusted manufacturing capability, while metal-on-ultra-thin-oxide etch introduced new and less well-understood damage mechanisms.

### 2.2 Doping and Etch Rate

Floating gate polysilicon is typically in-situ or implant doped (commonly n-type, phosphorus or arsenic) to a degree sufficient to behave as a reasonable conductor for capacitive coupling purposes, though its doping level and grain structure are optimized primarily for process compatibility and film stress rather than for minimizing sheet resistance (unlike the control gate, which carries the word line signal and is doping/silicide-optimized for conductivity). Doping level affects plasma etch rate measurably in halogen-based chemistries — more heavily doped polysilicon etches somewhat faster in Cl₂- and HBr-based chemistries due to enhanced reaction kinetics at grain boundaries and dopant-assisted chemical etching — which means floating gate etch recipes must be characterized against the specific doping profile used in production, not against a generic "polysilicon" etch rate assumption.

### 2.3 Grain Structure and Line Edge Roughness

CVD polysilicon forms a granular microstructure (grain sizes typically tens of nanometers, depending on deposition temperature and subsequent thermal budget) rather than a continuous single-crystal film. During etch, grain boundaries etch preferentially relative to grain interiors in many chemistries, because grain boundaries have higher defect density and different local doping/strain, which means the as-etched sidewall is not perfectly smooth at the scale of the grain size — it has roughness correlated with the underlying grain structure. At relaxed pitch this roughness is a small fraction of the critical dimension and is not consequential; at advanced pitch (Chapter 10), grain-boundary-correlated roughness becomes a measurable fraction of an already-shrinking CD budget.

## Part 3: ONO Interpoly Dielectric

### 3.1 Why a Three-Layer Stack

The interpoly dielectric separating floating gate from control gate is conventionally a three-layer oxide-nitride-oxide (ONO) stack rather than a single oxide layer, for a specific electrical reason: a single oxide thick enough to block charge leakage between the two polysilicon layers at the required voltage would reduce the coupling ratio (Chapter 1, Chapter 4) below a usable value, because oxide alone has a comparatively low dielectric constant (~3.9) and the geometric capacitance it provides falls as the oxide gets thicker. Inserting a higher-dielectric-constant nitride layer (silicon nitride, Si₃N₄, dielectric constant ~7) between two thin oxide layers allows the stack to present higher total capacitance (preserving coupling ratio) at a given leakage-blocking physical thickness, because the nitride layer both raises the effective dielectric constant of the stack and physically interrupts defect percolation paths that might otherwise connect a pinhole in the bottom oxide to a pinhole in the top oxide.

### 3.2 Typical Layer Thicknesses and Oxide-Equivalent Thickness

| Layer | Typical Physical Thickness | Role |
|-------|------------------------------|------|
| **Bottom oxide** | 3–5 nm | Blocks charge injection from floating gate into nitride traps; interfaces to FG polysilicon |
| **Nitride** | 4–6 nm | Raises effective dielectric constant; interrupts defect percolation path |
| **Top oxide** | 3–5 nm | Blocks charge injection from control gate; interfaces to CG polysilicon |
| **Oxide-equivalent thickness (EOT)** | ~10–15 nm | Combined electrical thickness used in coupling ratio and leakage calculations |

### 3.3 Etch-Relevant Properties

The ONO stack is the single most chemically distinct step in the floating gate etch sequence (Chapter 7), because it requires a plasma chemistry capable of etching both oxide and nitride with controlled, predictable relative rates, sandwiched between two polysilicon etch steps that use entirely different (halogen-dominant, as opposed to fluorocarbon-dominant) chemistries. Oxide and nitride etch via different volatile byproducts and different ion-assisted mechanisms, and a chemistry tuned to etch nitride efficiently may etch oxide too slowly (or vice versa), producing an ONO breakthrough step that is disproportionately slow, disproportionately damage-prone, or prone to leaving residue — any of which complicates the transition into the subsequent floating gate polysilicon etch.

## Part 4: Control Gate Polysilicon

### 4.1 Role and Doping

The control gate forms the word line — the conductor that carries the programming, read, and erase voltages to every cell along a row simultaneously. Unlike the floating gate, the control gate is electrically contacted (at the array periphery) and must carry current with acceptably low resistance across the full length of a word line, which can span the width of an entire block. Control gate polysilicon is therefore doped as heavily as process compatibility allows, and is nearly always capped with a lower-resistivity conductor (Part 5) because polysilicon alone, even heavily doped, has sheet resistance too high to meet word line RC delay targets at advanced pitch.

### 4.2 Etch-Relevant Properties

Control gate polysilicon etch (Chapter 6) is, in isolation, the most "conventional" step in the sequence — closely related to generic logic gate polysilicon etch chemistry (HBr/Cl₂/O₂-based systems), because at this point in the stack there is no tunnel-oxide-proximal damage concern (the ONO layer beneath still has to be etched, providing some protective margin). The principal etch engineering concerns at this step are profile control (since this is typically the thickest single polysilicon layer in the stack, and profile errors introduced here propagate downward) and clean transition into the silicide/metal cap etch above it and the ONO etch below it.

## Part 5: Silicide/Metal Cap

### 5.1 Why a Cap Is Needed

Polysilicon's sheet resistance, even heavily doped, is typically in the tens of ohms per square — adequate for short interconnects but producing unacceptable RC delay across a full word line at advanced pitch, where word line length can be hundreds of micrometers to low millimeters and line width has shrunk to tens of nanometers. A refractory metal silicide — historically most commonly tungsten silicide (WSix) in NAND word line stacks, though cobalt and titanium silicides saw use in adjacent logic/DRAM contexts — is formed or deposited on top of the control gate polysilicon specifically to reduce sheet resistance by roughly an order of magnitude, bringing word line RC delay back within acceptable bounds for array read/program timing.

### 5.2 Etch-Relevant Properties

| Property | Behavior | Etch Consequence |
|----------|----------|-------------------|
| **Composition** | WSix (x typically 2–3) | Requires fluorine-containing or chlorine-containing chemistry distinct from pure polysilicon etch optimum |
| **Hardness/sputter yield** | Comparable to or somewhat higher than polysilicon | Mask erosion budget must account for this step separately from the polysilicon steps that follow |
| **Byproduct volatility** | WFx and WClx species have different volatility than SiFx/SiClx | Residue and redeposition behavior differs from the polysilicon steps; can affect sidewall passivation balance |
| **Interface to control gate poly** | Must transition cleanly without leaving conductive stringers | A silicide stringer bridging adjacent word lines is an immediate electrical short, distinct from (but related to) the polysilicon stringer mechanisms of Chapter 11 |

## Part 6: Hard Mask (Preview)

A hard mask (most commonly a deposited oxide, nitride, or amorphous carbon layer, or a multi-layer combination) sits atop the entire stack described above, patterned by lithography and used to transfer the cell pattern into the underlying film stack via etch, since photoresist alone generally lacks sufficient etch resistance and thickness budget to survive the full six-step etch sequence described in Chapter 1. Hard mask material selection, and the specific challenges of maintaining adequate mask selectivity across five chemically distinct etch steps in sequence, is developed in full in Chapter 5; it is mentioned here only to complete the inventory of materials the etch process must contend with, from the top of the stack to the bottom.

## Chapter Summary

- Tunnel oxide is a thermally grown, 6–9 nm SiO₂ layer operating with essentially zero thickness margin against etch-induced thinning or damage
- Floating gate polysilicon's doping and grain structure affect both its baseline etch rate and the line edge roughness transferred into the final profile
- The ONO interpoly dielectric is a three-layer oxide-nitride-oxide stack whose etch (Chapter 7) is the most chemically distinct step in the sequence, bridging two polysilicon-dominant etch regimes
- Control gate polysilicon etch is the most "conventional" step in the sequence but still constrains profile for everything beneath it
- The silicide/metal cap reduces word line sheet resistance by roughly an order of magnitude, at the cost of introducing a chemically distinct etch step and a distinct stringer/bridging failure mode
- Every material transition in this stack is also a process chemistry transition, which is the structural reason Part II of this book is organized material-by-material rather than treating "floating gate etch" as a single undifferentiated process step

## Study Questions

1. Why does the ONO interpoly dielectric use a three-layer oxide-nitride-oxide structure instead of a single thicker oxide layer, and what tradeoff does this structure resolve?
2. Explain why tunnel oxide thickness represents a compromise between programming voltage requirements and retention requirements, and why this leaves little margin for etch-induced thinning.
3. Why might floating gate polysilicon doping level be optimized differently than control gate polysilicon doping level, and how does this affect etch recipe transferability between the two steps?
4. A silicide stringer bridging two word lines and a polysilicon stringer bridging two floating gates are both "stringer" defects. Why are they nonetheless distinct failure mechanisms requiring potentially different mitigation strategies?
