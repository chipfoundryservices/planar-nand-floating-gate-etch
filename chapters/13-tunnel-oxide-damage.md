# Chapter 13: Tunnel Oxide Damage Mechanisms & Plasma-Induced Charging

## Executive Summary

This chapter develops, in direct mechanistic detail, the damage pathways that previous chapters have repeatedly referenced but not yet fully explained: how plasma exposure actually degrades tunnel oxide, distinct from the simple physical thinning (material removal) concern that has been this book's shorthand for selectivity requirements since Chapter 8. Plasma-induced charging damage, ultraviolet/vacuum-ultraviolet (UV/VUV) photon-induced trap generation, and direct ion bombardment damage are mechanistically distinct, respond differently to process parameter changes, and together form the physical basis for the retention and endurance degradation mechanisms introduced in Chapter 4.

---

## Part 1: Plasma-Induced Charging Damage

### 1.1 The Antenna Effect

Plasma processing exposes conductive structures on the wafer (here, control gate and floating gate polysilicon) to spatially and temporally non-uniform ion and electron fluxes, particularly within high-aspect-ratio features or at pattern-density transitions, where local charge collection on an exposed conductor can differ measurably between its top surface (generally net positively charged, from a plasma sheath's characteristically higher ion flux relative to electron flux at the wafer surface) and more shielded regions. A conductor connected to a thin gate oxide — here, the floating gate's connection to tunnel oxide — acts as an "antenna," collecting charge over its own exposed area and funneling the resulting current through the comparatively tiny oxide area beneath it, producing a locally very high effective current density through the oxide relative to what the bare plasma flux at the wafer surface alone would suggest.

### 1.2 Antenna Ratio and Its Relevance Here

The "antenna ratio" (conductor area exposed to plasma, divided by the gate oxide area that conductor connects to) is a standard figure of merit in plasma charging damage analysis; the floating gate, being a comparatively large-area conductor (at least relative to the tunnel oxide area directly beneath it, though both shrink together with scaling) connected to an extremely thin oxide, is intrinsically a high-antenna-ratio structure for essentially its entire fabrication history, which is one specific, quantifiable reason tunnel oxide charging damage risk is not a minor or occasional concern in floating gate etch, but a structurally built-in characteristic of the device being manufactured.

### 1.3 Why This Differs from Simple Overetch-Driven Thinning

Charging damage can occur even when the etch process has excellent selectivity and essentially no measurable tunnel oxide thickness loss, because it is driven by current flow and the resulting trap generation within the oxide (and at its interfaces), not by physical material removal. This is a critical distinction for process engineers: a process characterized solely by post-etch oxide thickness metrology (confirming no measurable thinning) can still be generating significant charging-induced trap density, which will not appear in a thickness measurement but will appear, later, in a retention or endurance qualification test (Chapter 4).

## Part 2: UV/VUV Photon-Induced Damage

### 2.1 Plasma as a Light Source, Not Just a Particle Source

Plasma discharges emit substantial ultraviolet and vacuum-ultraviolet radiation, generated by excited-state relaxation of plasma species (both the intentional process gases and any dissociation/excitation products), with photon energies in some cases sufficient to directly generate electron-hole pairs within the tunnel oxide or break Si-O bonds at the oxide/silicon interfaces, independent of any ion bombardment or charge collection mechanism. Because this radiation is not charge-carrying, it is not blocked or attenuated by the antenna/charge-collection considerations of Part 1 — it can reach and damage the tunnel oxide even through structures or geometries that would otherwise be considered "protected" from direct ion or charging exposure.

### 2.2 Why This Matters Specifically Near End-of-Clear

UV/VUV exposure of tunnel oxide becomes most consequential specifically during the final clearing and soft-landing/overetch period of the floating gate etch (Chapter 8), when tunnel oxide first becomes directly exposed to the plasma environment (rather than shielded beneath still-present floating gate polysilicon). This is a second, independent reason (beyond the ion-energy/selectivity argument already developed in Chapter 8) that soft-landing chemistry and conditions deserve dedicated characterization: a chemistry and plasma condition optimized purely for ion-energy-driven selectivity might still generate problematic UV/VUV flux, since the two damage mechanisms, while both present during the same process step, are not driven by identical plasma parameters and do not necessarily improve or worsen in lockstep with each other.

## Part 3: Direct Ion Bombardment Damage

### 3.1 Trap Generation Independent of Net Etching

Even ions that do not contribute to net material removal (for example, ions arriving after the local film has already cleared, striking exposed tunnel oxide directly, as discussed in Chapter 8's soft-landing context) can generate bulk and interface traps in the oxide through simple energetic collision cascades, broadly analogous to (though with different specific defect signatures than) the damage mechanisms well-studied in the ion implantation literature for other semiconductor process contexts, scaled down to the comparatively low ion energies typical of a tuned soft-landing etch step.

### 3.2 Why Reduced Ion Energy Helps, But Has a Floor

Chapter 9 already established that reduced bias power/ion energy improves both etch selectivity and damage minimization simultaneously during soft-landing. This chapter adds the important caveat that this improvement does not continue indefinitely as ion energy is reduced toward zero: sufficiently low ion energy eventually becomes inadequate to maintain the sidewall passivation clearing and polymer removal functions (Chapter 6, Chapter 7) that anisotropic, residue-free etch depends on, meaning there is a practical floor below which further ion energy reduction trades a damage benefit for a renewed stringer/residue risk (directly connecting back to Chapter 11). Soft-landing process development is therefore a genuine optimization between these two risks, not simply "as low ion energy as achievable."

## Part 4: Characterizing and Correlating Damage to Device Outcomes

### 4.1 Why Thickness Metrology Alone Is Insufficient

As established in Part 1.3, standard post-etch thickness metrology does not detect charging or UV/VUV-induced trap generation directly. Process characterization for tunnel oxide damage therefore typically requires dedicated electrical test structures — capacitors or simple transistor structures specifically designed and placed (often in scribe-line or dedicated test-chip regions) to allow direct measurement of oxide leakage current, charge-to-breakdown, or trap density via established oxide reliability test methodologies, run on wafers processed through the actual production floating gate etch sequence (or a representative subset of it) rather than on blanket, unpatterned test films alone, since antenna-ratio-dependent charging damage (Part 1.2) specifically requires a realistic, patterned structure to manifest.

### 4.2 Connecting Back to Retention and Endurance

The trap density and leakage current characteristics measured via the test structures in Part 4.1 are the physical quantities that directly determine the trap-assisted-tunneling and stress-induced-leakage-current retention/endurance degradation mechanisms introduced in Chapter 4. This chapter closes the causal chain this book has built since Chapter 1: etch process and chemistry decisions (Chapters 6–9) produce measurable trap density and leakage characteristics (this chapter), which directly determine the retention and endurance behavior (Chapter 4) that ultimately defines whether a floating gate NAND product meets its specification — a chain that must be understood and managed as a single, connected system, not as a sequence of separately-optimized engineering disciplines.

## Chapter Summary

- Plasma-induced charging damage arises from the floating gate's intrinsically high antenna ratio (large conductor area relative to thin oxide area beneath it), funneling collected plasma charge through tunnel oxide as locally concentrated current
- UV/VUV photon-induced damage is a mechanistically independent pathway, not blocked by charge-collection shielding, and is particularly relevant during the soft-landing/overetch period when tunnel oxide first becomes directly exposed
- Direct ion bombardment can generate traps even without net material removal, and reduced ion energy has a practical floor below which further reduction trades damage benefit for renewed stringer/residue risk
- Standard post-etch thickness metrology cannot detect charging or photon-induced damage; dedicated electrical test structures measuring leakage current, charge-to-breakdown, or trap density on realistically patterned structures are required
- This chapter completes the causal chain connecting etch process decisions through measurable oxide damage characteristics to the retention and endurance outcomes established in Chapter 4
